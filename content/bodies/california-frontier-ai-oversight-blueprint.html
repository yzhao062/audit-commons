What the Report Recommends

On November 18, 2026, Governor Gavin Newsom’s office released “AI Safety in 2026 and Beyond,” a report dated November 16 from the California Government Operations Agency (GovOps). GovOps wrote it under Executive Order N-9-26 as recommendations for the Legislature, not enacted law.

The central proposal is that every large frontier developer must “submit to ongoing oversight by licensed independent verification organizations” (IVOs). IVOs, the report says, “must have full access to the people and information they need to run tests” and share their “unredacted assessments with the state.” GovOps would license IVOs under the criteria in SB 813 and revoke licenses of those not operating with “sufficient expertise, independence, and rigor.”

Metrics, Incident Reports, and Shutdown Plans

On automating AI research and development (AI R&D), the report says developers should report “key metrics on their automation of AI R&D,” including the share of compute devoted to it, and that “the very first responsibility of the IVOs should be to verify these metrics.” It also recommends that the state hold standby authority to cap automated AI R&D, but not impose a cap immediately.

On incidents, the report says the current deadline for most critical safety incidents is 15 days and recommends 48 hours, keeping 24 hours for imminent catastrophic risks. Models capable of actions that could result in catastrophic risk would also face reporting duties even if trained with fewer than 10^26 integer or floating-point operations. Relying on that threshold alone, the report says, has kept incidents such as the OpenAI Hugging Face incident out of scope.

For emergencies, each large frontier developer would create a Risk Containment Protocol (RCP) that is “tested and verified on an ongoing basis by an IVO.” An RCP must show the ability to intervene at several levels, including compute, agents, internal access, and model deployment. Developers should test whether a model would try to block a shutdown, for example by copying its weights. State officials could also order an immediate shutdown when a model poses an imminent risk of a catastrophic event or major loss-of-control event that the developer cannot or will not address, effective during any court challenge.

Limits and Open Questions

The report states, “We do not address reforming liability law,” and gives “only brief attention to open-weight models.” It refers to “fees that model developers pay for IVOs” and says SB 813 bars IVOs from accepting payment tied to their results. On rotating IVOs among companies, having the government assign them, and pooling payment, it says, “We do not offer an opinion on these ideas here,” and recommends that the Legislature examine them.

We did not review the incidents the report cites. The report also asks that IVO reports state the level of access granted, how conflicts of interest were handled, and whether assessors could freely publish. Our editorial reading: an auditor should hold any future IVO report to that list and ask how the IVO was assigned and paid, which the report leaves partly open.
