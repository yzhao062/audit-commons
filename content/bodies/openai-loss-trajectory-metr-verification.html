What OpenAI Disclosed

On November 22, 2026, OpenAI Chief Scientist Jakub Pachocki published “Perplexity and Intelligence”, and on November 24 METR posted an analysis of OpenAI's published data. In the post, Pachocki argues for perplexity (whose logarithm is cross-entropy loss, with lower values indicating better predictions) as a metric for comparing model developers and informing shared safety standards. OpenAI says its primary held-out datasets are “sourced from our internal codebase” and the numbers are “for a fixed snapshot”.

Pachocki says that once OpenAI's losses fell below 0.8, it saw “the emergence of transformative capabilities in fields like cybersecurity and mathematics”. He expects “further transformative capabilities as we enter the ~0.68-0.7 log-perplexity window”, which OpenAI may not be testing for today, and “further degradation in currently dominant forms of alignment auditing”.

Pachocki proposes “common limits, based on perplexity and other measurements”, that developers commit not to cross until the ability to understand and monitor AI behavior reaches “a certain concrete milestone”, such as “a successor to CoT monitors”. Our November 26 read of the full post found no numeric limit. The ~0.68-0.7 range marks only where he expects new capabilities.

What METR Says and Asks For

METR says OpenAI published base-model log-perplexity for 13 models since GPT-3.5, and reads OpenAI's plot as showing the loss “falling about seven times faster” since early 2026 than from 2022 through 2025. Its staff are still discussing how to reconcile this with benchmark scores for OpenAI models, which METR says lack a comparable acceleration. On how much the data should change capability forecasts, METR “does not have a consensus view”, and it says the data may overstate true progress, for example through overfitting.

METR encourages all developers to publish such data. It says three items “would be especially informative”: (i) log-perplexity on other held-out corpora, which could eventually be “maintained by a third party”; (ii) the rough date each pretraining run ended; and (iii) changes over time in pretraining compute, labor spending, and experiment compute. Microsoft has since done the same for its own models using OpenAI's dataset, METR notes, and the linked post on X calls access to that dataset “a special case”.

Limits of the Evidence

In our November 26 read of both pages, neither reports an outside measurement of an OpenAI model, so the curve, like Microsoft's figures, is self-reported on text Audit Commons has not seen.

Pachocki notes that perplexity misses “advances outside of pretraining”, most notably chain-of-thought reasoning. OpenAI attributes the 2026 acceleration “mostly” to long-term research and compute investment and “only in a smaller part” to automation, while noting “meaningful design contributions coming from AI” in recent models. METR says pretraining end dates would help show how much AI uplift contributed.

Our editorial reading: a perplexity-based limit is easier to audit when outsiders can measure models on held-out text the lab does not control and know when each run ended.
