What the Bill Would Require

On November 24, 2026, Representative Josh Gottheimer (D-NJ) introduced H.R. 11157, the RSI Safety Act of 2026, which would set up a disclosure program on the automation of frontier AI research. Representative Nathaniel Moran (R-TX) is the original cosponsor, and the House referred the bill that day to the Committee on Science, Space, and Technology.

A new Commerce Department office, the Office of Frontier Artificial Intelligence Research, would run the program. Developers would be covered once they have used, or determined they intend to use, not less than 10 septillion integer or floating-point operations (1025, by our conversion) to train a model and have spent not less than $100,000,000 on training in the preceding 12 months. After consulting CAISSI, Commerce could adjust both numbers or, after one year, replace the operations test with capability-based criteria. The bill defines CAISSI as the Center for Advancing Innovation and Standards for Super Intelligence, a successor entity, or, if neither exists, Commerce advisers.

Reports, Notices, and Audits

Each quarter, a covered developer would file a report, due within 45 days after the quarter ends, on four listed categories of research work, and Commerce could add more by regulation. One is safety, evaluation, and oversight operations: AI systems used to design or conduct evaluations, red-teaming, monitoring, interpretability research, and review of other systems' outputs or actions. Each category would carry measures such as the share of computing AI systems performed substantially autonomously, the share of that work's outputs a human examined substantively, and an assessment against a meaningful-oversight test that Commerce would establish.

A developer that discovers or implements a material shift in research automation would have four business days to report it. Commerce would define material shifts by regulation, with a change from human-supervised to closed-loop operation as one example.

Within two years after the first report, and then at intervals of no more than two years, Commerce would audit the records a developer used to prepare its reports, with a final audit after reporting ends. A Commerce employee would examine the records on site or, if access is denied, under subpoena.

Confidentiality, Timing, and Limits

Filings would be confidential and exempt from the Freedom of Information Act, with one exception. Each report would include a summary of the safety, evaluation, and oversight category, which Commerce must publish within 30 days of receipt. Registration and reporting duties would start one year after enactment.

This is introduced legislation. GovInfo's bill status record, last updated November 25, lists the November 24 referral as the latest action, and the congress.gov bill page refused our automated request. Gottheimer's release credits no outside proposal and notes endorsement by Americans for Responsible Innovation (ARI). We covered ARI's October 26 proposal earlier.

Our editorial reading: developers would report these figures themselves, Commerce would check them against records, and filings would stay confidential except for developer-prepared summaries of one reporting category, so outsiders could compare developers without verifying their numbers.
