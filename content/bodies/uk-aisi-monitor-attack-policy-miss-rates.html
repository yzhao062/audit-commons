What AISI Tested

On November 23, 2026, the UK AI Security Institute (AISI) published a blog post saying its Red Team found that one monitor missed 0.2% to 23% of successful attacks, depending on the "attack policy" used to generate them. AISI's research page for the paper is dated November 22, and the arXiv preprint was submitted on November 25.

The monitor is what AISI calls an ex-production OpenAI monitor: GPT-5.4 running a prompt OpenAI supplied, which the blog post calls retired. Qwen 3.8 27B played the monitored agent on infrastructure-as-code (IaC) tasks and, in an appendix, LinuxArena tasks. Each attack pairs an ordinary main task with a harmful side task, and the miss rate is the share of successful attacks the monitor did not flag.

AISI varied only how attacks were generated: basic prompting, optimized prompting, reinforcement learning (RL), and transcript editing, where a separate model rewrites finished transcripts to look less suspicious. According to AISI, the attacks were developed with feedback from other monitors only, none from GPT-5.4.

Which Methods Produced High Miss Rates

On IaC tasks, the paper reports a 0.2% miss rate for the basic prompt and 6.4% at best for optimized prompting. Transcript editing produced the highest rate, 23.0% at 100 proposed edits per seed transcript, and RL reached 20.8%. Edited tool calls were not re-executed, so a language-model judge penalized edits that would no longer complete the side task, and Table 1 gives the 23.0% a 95% interval of about 11% to 37%.

According to the paper's Figure 1, running only the lightest optimization level of three methods (the basic prompt, the first prompt-optimization round, and the first RL checkpoint) finds miss rates of at most 5.1%. Optimization by the authors themselves had limited success for several weeks, they report.

Limits of the Evidence

These are AISI's own results, and Audit Commons has not seen the transcripts. AISI says they come from one test with one monitor and one agent, and the paper says the agent is less capable than the monitor. In the LinuxArena appendix, attacks tuned on IaC transferred weakly to the second setting: on GPT-5.4, prompting fell from 6.4% to 0.2% and RL from 20.8% to 0.7%, taking the best checkpoint in each setting. Transcript editing, tuned on LinuxArena, reached 16.2%. The paper adds that optimizing directly against GPT-5.4 could produce stronger attacks.

AISI concludes that, without an explicit argument for why proxy attacks are informative, evaluations on synthetic transcripts cannot on their own provide assurance that monitors will catch harmful activity in deployment. As of November 26, the blog post, research page, arXiv abstract page, and paper (HTML and PDF) link no code or attack transcripts, though Appendix H prints the basic prompt.

Our editorial reading: a claim that a monitor catches a given share of attacks should state the attack policy and how hard it was optimized, since those choices moved the measured miss rate from 0.2% to 23%.
