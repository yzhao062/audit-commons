What Apollo Reports

On November 23, 2026, Apollo Research published a report on what it calls Handoff: frontier AI companies' ongoing delegation of all AI research and development (R&D) functions, including high-level decisions, to internally deployed AI agents. The report splits Handoff into work execution, review, and decision-making.

Apollo calls work execution nearly fully delegated, and Anthropic says Claude writes over 90% of the changes merged into its core codebase, with human guidance. For review, Apollo estimates that less than 0.01% of internal agent activity reaches dedicated human safety review. That share is Apollo's own arithmetic from company counts: OpenAI's March 2026 post reports about 1,000 escalations, many from deliberate red-teaming, among tens of millions of internal coding trajectories, and Anthropic says its offline monitor flags one or two transcripts in every thousand, roughly 100,000 a week, of which about 50 reach a human. Anthropic adds that humans review, within a week, any action its online monitor blocks.

Decision-making is the least advanced part, Apollo says, since public sources indicate that consequential go/no-go decisions remain under human control. Citing Anthropic, Apollo adds that Claude "leads" 26% of Anthropic's AI R&D work as of August 2026, up from under 1% in February. Anthropic defines "leads" as Claude completing most of a task end to end under human supervision.

What Apollo Proposes

Apollo recommends that the Center for Advancing Innovation and Standards for Super Intelligence (CAISSI) set up a task force to develop a Handoff transparency standard. Companies would report against it to a government authority such as the Department of Commerce, or in the transparency or safety and security reports that existing and proposed legislation calls for. Third-party or government assessors could independently assess their practices and reporting.

Apollo proposes two layers of candidate metrics. The first layer, for review, pairs a miss rate for deliberately seeded flaws that bypass both AI monitors and human reviewers with the summed delay from agent activity to flag, review, and mitigation. No comparable test of human reviewers behind AI monitors has yet been run or publicly shared, Apollo says. A second layer, for decision-making, covers automation levels per task category, agents' discretionary experiment compute, a list of decisions reserved for humans, how often humans override seeded flawed proposals, and any reserved decision an agent made anyway.

Limits of the Evidence

Apollo writes that almost everything known about Handoff comes from the companies, "disclosed voluntarily, sporadically, and each by its own metrics." Anthropic's review figures cover only its most-used internal platform, and its 26% comes from an index rated by Claude models, which Anthropic says could repeat the checked model's errors.

The proposed standard is a recommendation to CAISSI. Apollo says seeded-flaw tests may overstate real-world detection because both human reviewers and AI models behave differently when they suspect a test. As of November 26, neither the report text nor the body of Apollo's page mentions its November 16 pilot towards embedded evaluations with OpenAI (our coverage).

Our editorial reading: ask a lab how many seeded flaws its monitors and reviewers together miss, and treat today's review shares as Apollo's estimates from company-reported counts.
