What Switzerland Reported

On November 24, 2026, Switzerland's National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC, in German Bundesamt für Cybersicherheit, BACS) published an advisory saying it had confirmed, for the first time, cyberattacks in Switzerland carried out with the assistance of an AI agent. The attackers used so-called "Agentic Offensive Frameworks." In all attacks known to the NCSC, the agents led to a compromise of the victim's IT systems, and the affected organizations span at least two sectors.

On November 26, the Swiss broadcaster Schweizer Radio und Fernsehen (SRF) reported that Florian Schütz, whom it identifies as director of the BACS, told it there are currently five confirmed cases. SRF says the affected include three critical infrastructure operators, systems were compromised in four of the five cases, and it is still unclear who is behind the attacks.

What Wiz Says Reaches Victim Logs

On November 25, Wiz Research published an analysis of two agentic offensive frameworks, ARTEX and Cairn. Wiz says the frameworks give each sub-goal a numeric task counter, an Intent ID, and place it in the model's prompts and workspace. The model can then copy the number into the names of accounts, files, and cloud resources it creates on a target.

In one intrusion spanning AWS and Azure, Wiz says it found 36 Intent IDs on the AWS side and 34 on the Azure side. All came from one climbing sequence, which linked both to the same run although the identities had nothing in common. Microsoft's November 25 post on ARTEX adds that an empty hunting result is not proof that no access occurred when the underlying logs are not collected.

Limits of the Evidence

Wiz calls these Intent ID signals "lossy" and says pacing by them is "a corroborating signal" that cannot serve as standalone attribution. It lists four ways the signal breaks: the count climbs unevenly, unrelated installations collide in the same numeric ranges, operators reset or parallelize, and Cairn restarts at i001 for every project, so cross-incident pacing applies only to the ARTEX family. For ARTEX, Wiz adds that the counter separates installations, and one operator can run several. Its November 23 post says its detections for this activity, including one for a step counter, are "expected to be perishable."

The advisory names ARTEX and CyberStrikeAI only as examples of publicly accessible agents and does not say which framework was used in the Swiss cases. No source we read reports Intent IDs in Swiss logs. Victims remain unnamed. On the advisory page, as we checked it on November 26, no indicators appear, although it says the NCSC shares them with critical infrastructure operators. According to the advisory all known attacks led to compromise, while SRF reports four of five, and neither source says whether they count the same cases. Wiz says it has seen activity using ARTEX and similar tools since late September 2026. All figures are as reported by the authorities, SRF, and the vendors, and Audit Commons has tested none.

Our editorial reading: an Intent ID can corroborate that scattered entries belong to one agent run, but it is a perishable clue that needs other evidence before anyone names an operator.
