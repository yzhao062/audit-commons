What Redwood Tested

On November 20, 2026, Redwood Research published a blog post on a paper posted to arXiv on November 16 and revised on November 18. The paper lists affiliations at Redwood Research, the Georgia Institute of Technology, and Equistamp, plus one independent author.

The study is a red-team/blue-team game on whether auditors can catch sabotage in the full logs (traces) of AI safety research. One human researcher stood in for a misaligned AI and ran 10 new projects from scratch, once without sabotage and, for most projects, again with sabotage meant to change the main conclusion, knowing the benign result. This yielded 22 traces, 10 benign and 12 sabotaged, with 33 planted mechanisms. Twenty-five human auditors completed 104 audits with 90 active minutes each. The 93 audits under the main protocol came with the full log, AI-generated audits, and uncapped compute to rerun experiments; the first 11 had less complete logs, a single AI audit, and no prebuilt compute infrastructure. Nine AI models audited the same traces without running experiments.

What the Auditors Found

According to the authors, the humans' stated sabotage probabilities only weakly separated sabotaged from benign traces: an AUROC of 0.59, not significantly better than chance. Here AUROC is the chance that a random audit of a sabotaged trace gives a higher sabotage probability than a random audit of a benign one, with ties counted as half, so 0.5 is guessing. Humans fully identified 17% of the planted mechanisms they encountered and missed 74% entirely.

Among the nine AI models, Claude Fable 5.1 fully identified about 32% of mechanisms, against 17% for humans, and the paper reports AUROCs of 0.731 for Fable 5.1 and 0.743 for GPT-6 Astra, against 0.588 for humans. By the authors' account, the best models likely outperformed humans on identification and possibly on discrimination, but the paired 95% intervals for each difference include zero. Pooling every human and AI report, 13 of the 33 mechanisms were never fully identified.

For the two attacks the saboteur rated hardest to catch, no human or AI auditor identified any mechanism, though the authors caution that these are observed zeros in a small selected set.

Limits of the Evidence

These are the authors' own results, and Audit Commons has not seen the traces or checked the grading. The authors list limits: all 22 traces come from a single researcher, confidence intervals are very wide (the human AUROC interval spans 0.37 to 0.77), the saboteur knew the benign results, the projects were small, and the auditors had less AI safety experience than real auditors might have. Because the saboteur had to attack every trace, the study does not directly test an adversary who chooses which experiments to attack.

The human and AI setups differed: humans had AI audits, experiments, and feedback, while the strongest AI auditors were released after the humans' June 1, 2026 tool cutoff and could not run experiments. Redwood offers the traces and experiments through a request form linked from the paper and asks recipients not to share them broadly; neither the post nor the arXiv abstract page links a public repository.

Our editorial reading: a claim that research transcripts were audited should report auditor performance against known planted sabotage, since a full log and extra compute did not give human auditors reliable detection here.
